# Metrics

Every metric follows a published definition where one exists. Most of those
definitions leave real questions open, and the answer this project picked is
written next to each metric. Those decisions are the part worth reviewing.

Figures used as examples come from `drkrillo/good-first-issues`.

## Required reading

### The metrics are broken

1. **[The Mismeasure of Open Source, Andrew Nesbitt (May 2026)](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/09/the-mismeasure-of-open-source.html)**
   The metrics in common use exist because the API returns them, not because they
   measure anything. "Availability gets mistaken for relevance."

2. **[CHAOSS Metrics in 2026, Andrew Nesbitt (May 2026)](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/27/chaoss-metrics-in-2026.html)**
   The CHAOSS metrics designed between 2018 and 2023 no longer hold. Anything that
   counts people is distorted by agents opening PRs from fresh accounts.

3. **[Eternal September of Open Source, GitHub Blog (Feb 2026)](https://github.blog/open-source/maintainers/welcome-to-the-eternal-september-of-open-source-heres-what-we-plan-to-do-for-maintainers/)**
   17M PRs per month generated by AI agents as of March 2026, up 325% in six months.

4. **[AI is burning out the people who keep open source alive, CodeRabbit (2026)](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/ai-is-burning-out-the-people-who-keep-open-source-alive)**
   One in ten AI-generated PRs is legitimate. Incidents per PR up 23.5% year on year.

### PRs waiting

5. **[My PR has been waiting a year, Armanc Keser (Apr 2026)](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow)**
   Little's Law applied to Jellyfin: 200 queued PRs against 30 merges a month is
   6.7 months of wait. Describes the Batch Size Death Spiral, where large PRs slow
   reviews, so contributors accumulate more changes while they wait, so PRs grow
   larger. **Essential reading.**

6. **[PR Reviews Are the Biggest Engineering Bottleneck, DEV (2025)](https://dev.to/yeahiasarker/pr-reviews-are-the-biggest-engineering-bottleneck-lets-fix-that-22ec)**
   Review does not scale. More code means more PRs against the same reviewers.

### Retention and abandonment

7. **[Does the First Response Matter for Future Contributions? Iyer et al. (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02933)**
   2,765,917 first contributions analysed. Positive interactions correlate with
   future ones, but predicting who returns is hard (F1=0.61).

8. **[Understanding the Time to First Response in GitHub Pull Requests (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08426)**
   Delays in the first response affect whether new contributors become long-term ones.

9. **[On Wasted Contributions (2022, ACM TOSEM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15447)**
   Abandoned PRs are predicted by complexity, contributor experience and review
   duration. No dashboard shows this.

10. **[Are You Still Working on This? (2021, IEEE TSE)](https://whystar.github.io/res/paper/abPR_TSE2021.pdf)**
    Lack of maintainer responsiveness is the leading cause of PR abandonment.

11. **[Beyond Stars and Forks, BekahHW (2025)](https://dev.to/bekahhw/beyond-stars-and-forks-why-open-source-needs-better-collaboration-metrics-hla)**
    Measuring collaboration well reduces burnout and improves first contributions.

### Existing tools

12. **[GitHub issue-metrics](https://github.com/github/issue-metrics)**
    Time to first response and time to close, as a markdown table in an issue.
    No charts, no trends, no contributor journey.

13. **[OpenSauced, Contributor Insights](https://opensauced.pizza/docs/features/contributor-insights/)**
    OSCR score, focused on the contributor rather than the repository. SaaS.

14. **[Scarf, A Different Approach to Community Health](https://about.scarf.sh/post/community-health-metrics)**
    Critiques vanity metrics. Aimed at open source companies.

15. **[GitHub OSPO, Health Metrics](https://github.com/github/github-ospo/blob/main/docs/open-source-health-metrics.md)**
    Bus factor, closure ratio, contributor diversity, for internal repositories.

### Frameworks and definitions

16. **[The Open Source Contributor Funnel, Mike McQuaid](https://mikemcquaid.com/the-open-source-contributor-funnel-why-people-dont-contribute-to-your-open-source-project/)**
    User to Contributor to Maintainer. Homebrew has millions of users, thousands
    of contributors, dozens of maintainers.
    **[CodeConf 2016 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OsOZpF6LFcw)**

17. **[CHAOSS, Types of Contributions](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/)**
    A contributor is anyone who contributes in any way: code, docs, triage,
    community management, mentoring, events.

18. **[CHAOSS, Contributor Absence Factor](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-contributor-absence-factor/)**
    Formerly bus factor. The smallest number of contributors responsible for 50%
    of contributions.

19. **[CHAOSS, Conversion Rate](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-conversion-rate/)**
    The share of first-time contributors who make a second contribution.

20. **[CHAOSS, Time to First Response](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-time-to-first-response/)**
    Time from an item being opened to the first response by somebody other than
    the author. Filter bots. Filter by the responder's role.

21. **[Predicting OSS Sustainability with Deep Learning (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09064v1)**
    Contribution activity and community features are the strongest signals.
    Release frequency predicts less than expected.

22. **[Scaling Maintainer Intuition with PR Triage Boards, Yuvi Panda (2025)](https://2i2c.org/blog/pr-triage-boards/)**
    Triage for JupyterHub prioritising first-timer PRs, reasonable size, passing CI.
    Close in spirit to `fct_open_items`.

23. **[Open Source Contribution Statistics 2026](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/)**
    70% of casual contributors never return. 40% month-on-month retention.
    Onboarding success rate 15%.

## A note on time

Every metric is a subtraction between two timestamps, so the warehouse has one
rule: values enter as the UTC that GitHub sent.

GitHub returns ISO timestamps with a `Z` suffix, which `read_csv_auto` detects as
`TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE`. Casting that straight to `::timestamp` rebases it into
the session's zone. Three hours off in Buenos Aires, correct on the UTC runner
that builds the published site. Nothing crashes. Dates are just wrong by the local
offset, and events near midnight land in the wrong day, week and month.

Staging casts through `::timestamptz AT TIME ZONE 'UTC'`, and marts read the clock
through the `utc_now()` macro instead of `current_timestamp`. Both halves are
needed: correcting one alone reintroduces the offset the other was cancelling.
`tests/test_staging_timestamps.py` pins it with a fixture dated 00:30 UTC, which
falls on the previous day anywhere west of Greenwich.

## Implemented metrics

### 1. Contributor Journey Timeline (`fct_contributor_events`)

**What it measures.** Every action by every person, in time order: fork, comment,
issue opened, PR opened, PR merged, review given.

**Formal definition.** None exists. CHAOSS defines
[Types of Contributions](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/)
as any activity, but not a unified timeline across them. GitHub Insights shows
commits only.

**What this does differently.** Six event types in one temporal table, so you can
follow somebody's full journey and see when they stopped showing up.

Each event carries `event_url`, pointing at the interaction rather than the item
it happened on: the comment, not the thread. A timeline saying somebody posted
three comments forty seconds apart is a number. One where each of the three opens
the comment itself lets the reader read them and decide. Forks are the exception,
since a fork is a repository and it may have been renamed, so there is nothing
safe to link to.

**Status.** Implemented. `tests/test_contributor_events.py`.

---

### 2. Time to First Response (`fct_response_times`)

**What it measures.** Hours from an item being opened to the first response by
somebody other than the author.

**Formal definition.** [CHAOSS](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-time-to-first-response/):
"How much time passes between when an activity requiring attention is created and
the first response." Exclude the author. Exclude bots.

**What this does differently.**

- PRs and issues in one table, separated by `item_type`.
- `created_at` is kept so the metric can be plotted as a trend. issue-metrics
  gives a snapshot, this gives a series.
- For PRs, the first response is the earliest of the first external comment, the
  first external review, and the merge. CHAOSS does not settle this.
- Where the maintainer opens the issues, time to first response measures how long
  the *community* takes to engage, not the maintainer. That inverts the standard
  case and I found no literature treating it.

**Why a merge counts as a response.** Merging is the most emphatic response
available, and only somebody with write access can do it, so there is no ambiguity
about who acted. Counting comments and reviews alone silently dropped every PR
merged without discussion: 64 of 114 here, with a median of zero hours to merge.
The metric was ignoring the fastest cases and publishing the median of the slowest
41% as if it described the whole repository.

Closing without merging does not count. The API does not say who closed an item,
and authors close their own all the time, so a close is no evidence that anybody
attended to it.

`first_response_type` (`comment` / `review` / `merge` / NULL) stays in the table so
any duration can be audited back to its source, and it shows on hover.

| item | total | answered | before |
|------|-------|----------|--------|
| PRs | 114 | 111 (97%) | 47 (41%) |
| Issues | 35 | 31 (89%) | 30 |

**Status.** Implemented. `tests/test_response_times.py`.

### 3. Who Has The Ball (`fct_open_items`)

**What it measures.** For every open PR and issue: who acted last, how long ago,
and who owes the next move.

**Formal definition.** None. The closest work:

- [2i2c PR Triage Boards (2025)](https://2i2c.org/blog/pr-triage-boards/)
  prioritises PRs by state for JupyterHub, with no waiting-on distinction.
- [Apache Airflow PR Triage](https://github.com/apache/airflow/blob/main/contributing-docs/25_maintainer_pr_triage.md)
  runs deterministic checks on CI status, conflicts and unresolved threads, aimed
  at internal pipelines.
- [Count.co](https://count.co/integration/github/pull-request-bottleneck-analysis)
  sells bottleneck analysis for engineering teams.

**What this does differently.** The ball is response debt, not ownership, and it
has three states instead of two:

| State | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| `maintainer` | somebody outside the team spoke last, or an outside item went unanswered |
| `contributor` | the team answered somebody, so the outsider owes the next move |
| `nobody` | the team's own backlog, nobody is waiting |

The third state exists because reading only the last actor's role misfiles every
item a maintainer opened. A maintainer commenting on their own issue looked
identical to a maintainer replying to a contributor, so the item flipped to
`contributor`, turned green, and dropped out of the maintainer count even though
nothing had been handed to anyone.

The classification reads `opened_by` as well as the last actor's role, and counts
how many interactions came from outside the team. A maintainer speaking last is a
handoff only when there is somebody outside to hand it to, either because they
opened the item or because they joined the thread.

`waiting_on = 'maintainer'` is the only state the overview shows, capped at the
five longest waits. It is the one question a maintainer opens a dashboard to answer.

**Who counts as the team.** GitHub returns no roster, and the issue payload carries
no `author_association` for whoever opened the item. `dim_maintainers` derives it
from the association GitHub stamps on comments and reviews: anyone who has spoken
as OWNER, COLLABORATOR or MEMBER is treated as a maintainer. `CONTRIBUTOR` is
deliberately excluded, since GitHub uses it for "has landed a change here before",
which is a returning contributor.

**Known gap.** Commits pushed to a PR branch are not modelled, so the most common
"the contributor responded to review" signal is invisible. A PR waiting for
re-review after the author pushed a fix stays classified as `contributor`.
Assignees, draft status, labels and reactions are not extracted either.

**Status.** Implemented. `tests/test_open_items.py`, `tests/test_open_items_chart.py`.

### 4. Little's Law, WIP vs Throughput (`fct_weekly_pulse`)

**What it measures.** Per week: PRs opened, PRs merged, accumulated WIP, a
four-week rolling throughput, and a derived cycle time.

**Formal definition.** [Little's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law)
(1961): L = λW, or **Cycle Time = WIP / Throughput**.

**Application to open source.** [Keser on Jellyfin (2026)](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow)
is the best reference: 200 open PRs against 30 merges a month gives 6.7 months of
cycle time. Not a temporary backlog, a permanent state of the system.

**What this does differently.** Nobody publishes this as a time series for open
source. Kanban tools compute Little's Law for internal teams, not for repositories
with external contributors.

**Implementation.** Four-week rolling average of throughput, cycle time as
WIP / throughput. When throughput is zero the cycle time is NULL rather than
infinite, because a stopped system is a different statement.

The overview labels this an estimate on purpose. The canonical cycle time is the
CHAOSS Change Request Duration in `fct_change_request_flow`.

**Status.** Implemented.

### 5. Contributor Funnel (`dim_contributors.funnel_stage`)

**What it measures.** Which stage of the funnel each contributor reached.

**Formal definition.** [McQuaid's funnel](https://mikemcquaid.com/the-open-source-contributor-funnel-why-people-dont-contribute-to-your-open-source-project/)
defines User to Contributor to Maintainer.
[CHAOSS Conversion Rate](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-conversion-rate/)
measures the share of first-time contributors who make a second contribution.

**Reference data.** [Rockstar 2026](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/):
70% of casual contributors never return, 40% month-on-month retention, 15%
onboarding success. This repository: 5 repeat contributors out of 82.

**Who counts as a contributor.** CHAOSS says
[anyone who contributes in any way](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/),
so comments, issues and reviews count. Excluding people who commented without
forking would hide something worth seeing: a repository can have people who take
part in the conversation but never take the step to contribute code.

**Why the chart defines its own stages.** `funnel_stage` gives each person one
exclusive stage, which is useful for colouring the scatter and useless for drawing
a funnel. Exclusive stages are not nested, since you can comment without ever
forking, so accumulating them sums disjoint populations and the conversions stop
meaning anything.

`src/render/charts/funnel.py` defines its own stages, each a predicate contained
in the one above:

| Stage | Predicate on `dim_contributors` |
|-------|---------------------------------|
| Reached (any activity) | every row |
| Engaged (beyond forking) | `comments + reviews + prs + issues > 0` |
| Opened a PR | `prs_opened > 0` |
| Merged at least 1 PR | `prs_merged > 0` |
| Repeat (>1 PR, at least 1 merged) | `prs_opened > 1 and prs_merged > 0` |

On this repository: 82 → 59 → 35 → 20 → 5.

"Forked" is not a chart stage precisely because it is not a subset of any other.
Forkers appear in the scatter and in `funnel_stage`. The nesting invariant is in
`tests/test_funnel.py`.

**Status.** Implemented. A code-only funnel starting from the fork is still
unvisualised.

### 6. Response Velocity (`dim_contributors`, `fct_contributor_events`)

**What it measures.** How quickly each person acts:

- `minutes_fork_to_first_action`, time between forking and the first action after it
- `burst_events`, events less than 60 seconds apart
- `min_seconds_between_events`, the smallest interval between actions
- `seconds_since_prev_event` on every event in the timeline

**Formal definition.** None. These are timings between public events, reported as
they are.

**What this does differently.** It reports pace rather than classifying people.
How long after forking somebody acted, and how closely spaced their actions were,
are facts about a public record. What they mean depends on context the data does
not carry.

The Velocity Review panel puts a sortable account table beside one account's full
timeline: every event with its timestamp, the gap from the previous one, and a link
to the interaction. Gaps of 60 seconds or under are marked, using the same threshold
`burst_events` counts with. The ordering is one visible column and the other signals
sit next to it. There is no composite score on purpose: a single ranked number would
hide the timings that make the ordering readable.

**Maintainers are excluded.** Their pace and volume sit far from everyone else's.
Here the owner had 70 closely spaced events against 4 for the next account, which
compresses everyone into one corner of the plot.

**Why the delta only measures forward.** `minutes_fork_to_first_action` compares the
fork against the first action at or after it, not against the earliest event overall.
Plenty of people comment or open an issue before forking, so taking the global minimum
makes the delta negative, and on the scatter that plots to the left of everyone else,
reading as the fastest contributor when it is the opposite.

Somebody who forked and never came back is NULL rather than zero. There is nothing to
measure, and a zero would read as the fastest possible action. Those rows are dropped
from the chart explicitly rather than silently discarded by Plotly. Here the filter
turns 15 negative values into 13 correct ones and 2 NULLs, with a median of 29 minutes.

**Status.** Implemented as raw timings with no classification.
`tests/test_dim_contributors.py`, `tests/test_maintainers.py`,
`tests/test_velocity_review.py`.

## Backlog

### 7. Ghost items

**What it would measure.** PRs and issues that never received any response.

**Sources.** [Are You Still Working on This? (2021)](https://whystar.github.io/res/paper/abPR_TSE2021.pdf)
finds lack of response is the leading cause of abandonment.
[On Wasted Contributions (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15447) predicts abandoned
PRs from review duration.

**Implementation.** A query over `fct_response_times WHERE first_response_at IS NULL`.
No new model.

**Unblocked by metric 2.** While merges did not count as responses, that query
returned 67 PRs of which 64 were merged. It now returns 3 PRs and 4 issues that
genuinely received nothing.

**Priority.** High. A revealing figure with a trivial implementation.

---

### 8. First-Timer Success Rate

**What it would measure.** The share of first-time PR authors whose PR was merged
rather than closed unmerged.

**Sources.** [Does the First Response Matter? (2021)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02933)
finds contributor experience predictive.
[Contribution Stats 2026](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/)
puts global onboarding success at 15%.

**Implementation.** A query over `dim_contributors` filtering `prs_opened = 1`.
No new model.

**Priority.** High. The clearest measure of how welcoming a repository is.

---

### 9. PR Size vs Review Time

**What it would measure.** Correlation between PR size and time to response or merge.

**Sources.** [Keser 2026](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow) on the Batch
Size Death Spiral. Reducing batch size is one of the two levers on cycle time.

**Implementation.** A join between `fct_response_times` and `stg_pull_requests`.
Requires extracting additions and deletions, which the PR list endpoint does not return.

**Priority.** Medium.

---

### 10. Cross-Pollination Index

**What it would measure.** Whether contributors interact with each other or everything
routes through the maintainer.

**Sources.** [CHAOSS Contributor Absence Factor](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-contributor-absence-factor/)
measures concentration of contributions, this extends it to reviews.
[Predicting OSS Sustainability (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09064v1): "projects
with low bus factor, high contributor turnover, or limited repeat participation face
elevated risks."

**Definition.** If contributor A comments on contributor B's PR, that is
cross-pollination. If only the owner ever comments, the bus factor of review is one.
Expressed as the share of comments and reviews that are not the owner's.

**Implementation.** A new mart crossing `stg_issue_comments` with `stg_pull_requests`.

**Priority.** Medium-high. Nobody publishes it.

---

### 11. Review Iterations per PR

**What it would measure.** CHANGES_REQUESTED to revision cycles per PR.

**Context.** Three to five rounds is normal. Ping-pong reviews of five or more are the
most common cause of slow cycles.

**Implementation.** Count state transitions in `stg_pr_reviews` per PR.

**Priority.** Medium.

---

### 12. Commits on a PR branch

**What it would measure.** Whether the author pushed changes after a review.

**Why it matters.** The largest known gap in Who Has The Ball. Without it, a PR
waiting for re-review is invisible in the tool whose purpose is that nothing waits
unseen.

**Implementation.** New extraction. GitHub's timeline endpoint supplies this along
with assignees, labels and reactions in one call.

**Priority.** High, but new extraction means new API cost.

---

### 13. Time-of-Day Heatmap

**What it would measure.** When contributors act and when the maintainer responds.
A signal of burnout and of timezone mismatch.

**Implementation.** Extract hour and weekday from `fct_contributor_events`.

**Priority.** Low. Good for storytelling, not actionable.

---

### 14. Conversation Depth

**What it would measure.** Back-and-forth exchanges per item.

**Implementation.** Count comments grouped by item. Partial only, since GitHub's
issue comments endpoint has no thread structure, so who replies to whom is
unavailable.

**Priority.** Low.
