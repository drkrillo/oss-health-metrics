# Metrics — definitions, sources, and design decisions

Every metric here follows a published definition where one exists. Most of those
definitions leave real questions open, and the answers this project picked are
recorded alongside each metric. Those decisions are the part worth reviewing.

Figures quoted as examples come from `drkrillo/good-first-issues`.

## Required reading

The sources that set the context for this project, in order.

### The problem: the metrics are broken

1. **[The Mismeasure of Open Source — Andrew Nesbitt (May 2026)](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/09/the-mismeasure-of-open-source.html)**
   The metrics in common use exist because the API returns them, not because they
   measure anything useful. "Availability gets mistaken for relevance." Stars,
   forks and downloads say nothing about the health of a community.

2. **[CHAOSS Metrics in 2026 — Andrew Nesbitt (May 2026)](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/27/chaoss-metrics-in-2026.html)**
   The CHAOSS metrics designed between 2018 and 2023 no longer hold. AI agents
   distort New Contributors, Conversion Rate, and anything else that counts
   people. A bot opening PRs from fresh accounts registers as a burst of new
   contributors.

3. **[Eternal September of Open Source — GitHub Blog (Feb 2026)](https://github.blog/open-source/maintainers/welcome-to-the-eternal-september-of-open-source-heres-what-we-plan-to-do-for-maintainers/)**
   17M PRs per month generated by AI agents as of March 2026, a 325% rise in six
   months. Maintainers are drowning.

4. **[AI is burning out the people who keep open source alive — CodeRabbit (2026)](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/ai-is-burning-out-the-people-who-keep-open-source-alive)**
   Only one in ten AI-generated PRs is legitimate. Cortex 2026: incidents per PR
   up 23.5% year on year while PRs per author rose 20%.

### The bottleneck: PRs waiting

5. **[My PR has been waiting a year — Armanc Keser (Apr 2026)](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow)**
   Applies Little's Law to Jellyfin: 200 PRs queued against 30 merges a month is
   6.7 months of average wait. Describes the "Batch Size Death Spiral": large PRs
   slow reviews, work piles up, contributors add more while waiting, PRs grow
   larger. **Essential reading.**

6. **[PR Reviews Are the Biggest Engineering Bottleneck — DEV (2025)](https://dev.to/yeahiasarker/pr-reviews-are-the-biggest-engineering-bottleneck-lets-fix-that-22ec)**
   Review does not scale. More code means more PRs against the same number of
   reviewers.

### Contributors: retention and abandonment

7. **[Does the First Response Matter for Future Contributions? — Iyer et al. (2021, MSR / Springer 2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02933)**
   2,765,917 first contributions analysed. Positive interactions correlate with
   future contributions, but predicting whether someone returns is hard
   (F1=0.61) and depends on the project and the contributor, not the reply alone.

8. **[Understanding the Time to First Response in GitHub Pull Requests (2023, arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08426)**
   Delays in the first response significantly affect whether new contributors
   become long-term ones.

9. **[On Wasted Contributions (2022, ACM TOSEM)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15447)**
   Abandoned PRs are predicted by complexity, contributor experience and review
   duration. No dashboard shows this.

10. **[Are You Still Working on This? (2021, IEEE TSE)](https://whystar.github.io/res/paper/abPR_TSE2021.pdf)**
    Lack of maintainer responsiveness is the leading cause of PR abandonment.

11. **[Beyond Stars and Forks — BekahHW, DEV (2025)](https://dev.to/bekahhw/beyond-stars-and-forks-why-open-source-needs-better-collaboration-metrics-hla)**
    "When collaboration is measured effectively, it can reduce contributor
    burnout, increase successful first contributions, and build more sustainable
    projects."

### Existing tools and their gaps

12. **[GitHub issue-metrics](https://github.com/github/issue-metrics)**
    Time to first response and time to close, output as a markdown table in an
    issue. No charts, no trends, no contributor journey.

13. **[OpenSauced — Contributor Insights](https://opensauced.pizza/docs/features/contributor-insights/)**
    OSCR score, focused on the individual contributor rather than the health of a
    repository. SaaS.

14. **[Scarf — A Different Approach to Community Health](https://about.scarf.sh/post/community-health-metrics)**
    Critiques vanity metrics, aimed at open source companies rather than
    community-driven repositories.

15. **[GitHub OSPO — Health Metrics](https://github.com/github/github-ospo/blob/main/docs/open-source-health-metrics.md)**
    GitHub's framework for internal repositories: bus factor, closure ratio,
    contributor diversity.

### Frameworks and definitions

16. **[The Open Source Contributor Funnel — Mike McQuaid (Homebrew)](https://mikemcquaid.com/the-open-source-contributor-funnel-why-people-dont-contribute-to-your-open-source-project/)**
    Defines the stages User → Contributor → Maintainer. Homebrew has millions of
    users, thousands of contributors, dozens of maintainers; the drop-off between
    stages is enormous. **[CodeConf 2016 talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OsOZpF6LFcw)**

17. **[CHAOSS — Types of Contributions](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/)**
    A contributor is anyone who contributes to the project in any way: code,
    docs, triage, community management, mentoring, events. Not only code.

18. **[CHAOSS — Contributor Absence Factor](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-contributor-absence-factor/)**
    Formerly "bus factor". The smallest number of contributors responsible for
    50% of contributions. A low number means high risk.

19. **[CHAOSS — Conversion Rate](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-conversion-rate/)**
    The share of first-time contributors who make a second contribution. Nesbitt
    (2026) argues AI agents have broken this metric.

20. **[CHAOSS — Time to First Response](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-time-to-first-response/)**
    Time from a PR or issue being opened to the first response by somebody other
    than the author. Filter bots. Filter by the responder's role.

21. **[Predicting OSS Sustainability with Deep Learning (Feb 2026, arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09064v1)**
    Contribution activity and community features are the strongest signals of
    sustainability. Release frequency predicts less than expected.

22. **[Scaling Maintainer Intuition with PR Triage Boards — Yuvi Panda, 2i2c (Nov 2025)](https://2i2c.org/blog/pr-triage-boards/)**
    A triage system for JupyterHub prioritising first-timer PRs, reasonable size
    and passing CI. Close in spirit to `fct_open_items`.

23. **[Open Source Contribution Statistics 2026 — Rockstar Developer University](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/)**
    70% of casual contributors never return. 40% month-on-month retention for
    regular contributors. Onboarding success rate 15%.

## A note on time

Every metric here is a subtraction between two timestamps, so the warehouse has
one rule about them: values enter as the UTC that GitHub sent.

GitHub returns ISO timestamps with a `Z` suffix, which DuckDB's `read_csv_auto`
detects as `TIMESTAMP WITH TIME ZONE`. Casting that straight to `::timestamp`
rebases it into the session's zone — three hours off in Buenos Aires, correct on
the UTC runner that builds the published site. Nothing crashes; dates are simply
wrong by the local offset, and events near midnight bucket into the wrong day,
week and month.

Staging therefore casts through `::timestamptz AT TIME ZONE 'UTC'`, and marts
read the clock through the `utc_now()` macro rather than `current_timestamp`.
Both halves are needed: correcting one alone reintroduces the offset the other
was cancelling. `tests/test_staging_timestamps.py` pins this down with a fixture
dated 00:30 UTC, which falls on the previous day anywhere west of Greenwich.

## Implemented metrics

### 1. Contributor Journey Timeline (`fct_contributor_events`)

**What it measures.** Every action by every person in the repository, in time
order: fork, comment, issue opened, PR opened, PR merged, review given.

**Formal definition.** None exists. CHAOSS defines
[Types of Contributions](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/)
as any activity, but does not define a unified timeline across them. GitHub
Insights shows a per-contributor timeline of commits only, ignoring comments,
reviews, forks and issues.

**What this does differently.** Six event types unified into one temporal table,
which makes it possible to follow a contributor's full journey, compare the pace
of one person against another, and see when somebody stopped showing up.

Each event also carries `event_url`, pointing at the interaction itself rather
than the item it happened on — the comment, not the thread. A timeline saying
somebody posted three comments forty seconds apart is a number; one where each of
the three opens the comment itself lets the reader read them and draw their own
conclusion. Forks are the exception: a fork is a repository, and it may since have
been renamed, so there is nothing safe to link to.

**Status.** Implemented. Invariants in `tests/test_contributor_events.py`.

---

### 2. Time to First Response (`fct_response_times`)

**What it measures.** Hours from a PR or issue being opened to the first response
by somebody other than the author.

**Formal definition.** [CHAOSS — Time to First Response](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-time-to-first-response/):
"How much time passes between when an activity requiring attention is created and
the first response." Exclude the author's own actions. Exclude bots.

**What this does differently.**

- PRs and issues live in one table, separated by `item_type`.
- `created_at` is kept so the metric can be plotted as a trend. issue-metrics
  gives a snapshot; this gives a series.
- For PRs, the first response is the earliest of the first external comment, the
  first external review, **and the merge**. CHAOSS does not settle this.
- For repositories where the maintainer opens the issues, time to first response
  on issues measures how long the *community* takes to engage, not how long the
  maintainer takes to reply. That is an inversion of the standard case, and no
  literature was found treating the distinction.

**Why a merge counts as a response.** Merging is the most emphatic response
available, and only somebody with write access can do it, so there is no
ambiguity about who acted. Counting comments and reviews alone silently dropped
every PR merged without discussion — 64 of 114 here, with a median of zero hours
to merge. The metric was ignoring the fastest cases and publishing the median of
the slowest 41% as if it described the whole repository.

Closing **without** merging does not count. The API does not say who closed an
item, and authors close their own PRs and issues all the time, so a close is no
evidence that anybody attended to it.

`first_response_type` (`comment` / `review` / `merge` / NULL) stays in the table
so any duration can be audited back to its source, and it is shown on hover.

Coverage after the change:

| item | total | answered | before |
|------|-------|----------|--------|
| PRs | 114 | 111 (97%) | 47 (41%) |
| Issues | 35 | 31 (89%) | 30 |

**Status.** Implemented. Invariants in `tests/test_response_times.py`.

### 3. Who Has The Ball (`fct_open_items`)

**What it measures.** For every open PR and issue: who acted last, how long ago,
and who owes the next move.

**Formal definition.** No standard metric exists. The closest work:

- [2i2c PR Triage Boards (2025)](https://2i2c.org/blog/pr-triage-boards/):
  prioritises PRs by state for JupyterHub, without an explicit waiting-on
  distinction.
- [Apache Airflow PR Triage](https://github.com/apache/airflow/blob/main/contributing-docs/25_maintainer_pr_triage.md):
  deterministic checks on CI status, merge conflicts and unresolved threads,
  aimed at internal pipelines.
- [Count.co — PR Bottleneck Analysis](https://count.co/integration/github/pull-request-bottleneck-analysis):
  bottleneck analysis for engineering teams, sold as SaaS.

**What this does differently.** The ball is modelled as *response debt*, not
ownership, and it has three states rather than two:

| State | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| `maintainer` | somebody outside the team spoke last, or an outside item went unanswered |
| `contributor` | the team answered somebody; the outsider owes the next move |
| `nobody` | the team's own backlog — no outsider is involved, so no one is waiting |

The third state exists because reading only the last actor's role misfiles every
item a maintainer opened. A maintainer commenting on their own issue looks
identical to a maintainer replying to a contributor, so the item used to flip to
`contributor`, turn green, and drop out of the maintainer count even though
nothing had been handed to anyone.

The classification therefore reads `opened_by` as well as the last actor's role,
and counts how many interactions came from outside the team. A maintainer
speaking last is a handoff only when there is somebody outside to hand it to —
either because they opened the item or because they joined the thread.

`waiting_on = 'maintainer'` is the only state the overview shows, capped at the
five longest waits. It is the one question a maintainer opens a dashboard to
answer.

**Who counts as the team.** GitHub returns no roster, and the issue/PR payload
carries no `author_association` for whoever opened the item. `dim_maintainers`
derives it from the association GitHub stamps on comments and reviews: anyone who
has ever spoken as OWNER, COLLABORATOR or MEMBER on a repository is treated as a
maintainer of it. CHAOSS-style `CONTRIBUTOR` is deliberately excluded — GitHub
uses it for "has landed a change here before", which is a returning contributor,
not a maintainer.

**Known gap.** Commits pushed to a PR branch are not modelled, so the most common
"the contributor responded to review" signal is invisible. A PR waiting for
re-review after the author pushed a fix stays classified as `contributor`.
Assignees, draft status, labels and reactions are likewise not extracted.

**Status.** Implemented. Invariants in `tests/test_open_items.py` and
`tests/test_open_items_chart.py`.

### 4. Little's Law — WIP vs Throughput (`fct_weekly_pulse`)

**What it measures.** Per week: PRs opened, PRs merged, accumulated WIP, a
four-week rolling throughput, and a derived cycle time.

**Formal definition.** [Little's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law)
(1961): L = λW, or **Cycle Time = WIP / Throughput**. WIP is items in progress,
throughput is items completed per unit of time, cycle time is how long an item
spends in the system.

**Application to open source.** [Armanc Keser on Jellyfin (2026)](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow)
is the best reference: 200 open PRs against 30 merges a month gives 6.7 months of
cycle time. Not a temporary backlog — a permanent state of the system. The same
piece describes the Batch Size Death Spiral, where large PRs slow reviews, which
makes contributors accumulate more changes while they wait, which makes PRs
larger still.

**What this does differently.** Nobody publishes this as a time series for open
source repositories. Kanban and agile tools compute Little's Law for internal
teams, not for community repositories with external contributors.

**Implementation.** Four-week rolling average of throughput; cycle time as
WIP / throughput. When throughput is zero the cycle time is NULL rather than
infinite — the system is stopped, which is a different statement.

The overview labels this an estimate on purpose. The canonical cycle time is the
CHAOSS Change Request Duration in `fct_change_request_flow`.

**Status.** Implemented.

### 5. Contributor Funnel (`dim_contributors.funnel_stage`)

**What it measures.** Which stage of the funnel each contributor reached.

**Formal definition.** [Mike McQuaid's funnel](https://mikemcquaid.com/the-open-source-contributor-funnel-why-people-dont-contribute-to-your-open-source-project/)
defines User → Contributor → Maintainer.
[CHAOSS — Conversion Rate](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-conversion-rate/)
measures the share of first-time contributors who make a second contribution.

**Reference data.** [Rockstar Developer University 2026](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/):
70% of casual contributors never return, 40% month-on-month retention, 15%
onboarding success. This repository: 5 repeat contributors out of 82.

**Who counts as a contributor.** CHAOSS says
[anyone who contributes in any way](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-types-of-contributions/),
which includes comments, issues and reviews. This project counts all of them, not
only the people who forked. Excluding the ones who commented without forking
would hide something worth seeing: a repository can have people who take part in
the conversation but never take the step to contribute code.

**Why the chart defines its own stages.** `funnel_stage` assigns each person one
exclusive stage, which is useful for colouring the scatter and useless for
drawing a funnel: exclusive stages are **not nested** — you can comment without
ever forking — so accumulating them sums disjoint populations and the conversions
stop meaning anything.

`src/render/charts/funnel.py` defines its own stages, each computed from a
predicate and contained in the one above:

| Stage | Predicate on `dim_contributors` |
|-------|---------------------------------|
| Reached (any activity) | every row |
| Engaged (beyond forking) | `comments + reviews + prs + issues > 0` |
| Opened a PR | `prs_opened > 0` |
| Merged at least 1 PR | `prs_merged > 0` |
| Repeat (>1 PR, at least 1 merged) | `prs_opened > 1 and prs_merged > 0` |

On this repository: 82 → 59 → 35 → 20 → 5.

"Forked" is not a chart stage precisely because it is not a subset of any other.
Forkers appear in the scatter and in `funnel_stage`. The nesting invariant is
covered by `tests/test_funnel.py`.

**Status.** Implemented as a broad funnel with nested stages. A code-only funnel
starting from the fork is still unvisualised.

### 6. Response Velocity (`dim_contributors`, `fct_contributor_events`)

**What it measures.** How quickly each person acts:

- `minutes_fork_to_first_action` — time between forking and the first action
  **after** the fork
- `burst_events` — events less than 60 seconds apart
- `min_seconds_between_events` — the smallest interval between actions
- `seconds_since_prev_event` on every event in the timeline

**Formal definition.** No standard metric exists. These are timings between
public events, reported as they are.

**What this does differently.** It reports pace rather than classifying people.
How long after forking somebody acted, and how closely spaced their actions
were, are facts about a public record; what they mean depends on context the
data does not carry.

The Velocity Review panel puts a sortable account table beside one account's full
timeline: every event with its timestamp, the gap from the previous one, and a
link to the interaction. Gaps of 60 seconds or under are marked, using the same
threshold `burst_events` counts with. The ordering is one visible column and the
other signals sit next to it. There is deliberately no composite score — a single
ranked number would hide the reasoning that makes the underlying timings
readable, and the reader is better served seeing them.

**Maintainers are excluded.** Their pace and volume sit far from everyone else's.
On this repository the owner had 70 closely spaced events against 4 for the next
account, which compresses the rest of the population into one corner of the plot
and makes the visible range useless.

**Why the delta only measures forward.** `minutes_fork_to_first_action` compares
the fork against the first action that happens **at or after** it, not against
the earliest event overall. Plenty of people comment or open an issue before
forking; taking the global minimum makes the delta negative, and on the scatter
that plots to the left of everyone else — reading as the fastest contributor in
the repository when it is exactly the opposite.

Someone who forked and never came back is NULL rather than zero. There is nothing
to measure, and a zero would read as the fastest possible action. Those
contributors are dropped from the chart explicitly rather than silently discarded
by Plotly. On this repository the filter turns 15 negative values into 13 correct
ones and 2 NULLs, with a median of 29 minutes.

**Status.** Implemented as raw timings with no classification. Invariants in
`tests/test_dim_contributors.py`, `tests/test_maintainers.py` and
`tests/test_velocity_review.py`.

## Backlog

### 7. Ghost items

**What it would measure.** PRs and issues that never received any response.

**Sources.** [Are You Still Working on This? (2021)](https://whystar.github.io/res/paper/abPR_TSE2021.pdf)
finds lack of response is the leading cause of abandonment;
[On Wasted Contributions (2022)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15447) predicts
abandoned PRs from review duration.

**Implementation.** A query over `fct_response_times WHERE first_response_at IS
NULL`. No new model needed.

**Unblocked by metric 2.** While merges did not count as responses, that query
returned 67 PRs of which 64 were merged — noise. It now returns 3 PRs and 4
issues that genuinely received nothing.

**Priority.** High. A revealing figure with a trivial implementation.

---

### 8. First-Timer Success Rate

**What it would measure.** The share of first-time PR authors whose PR was merged
rather than closed unmerged.

**Sources.** [Does the First Response Matter? (2021/2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.02933)
finds contributor experience predictive;
[Open Source Contribution Stats 2026](https://rockstardeveloperuniversity.com/open-source-contribution-statistics/)
puts the global onboarding success rate at 15%.

**Implementation.** A query over `dim_contributors` filtering `prs_opened = 1`.
No new model needed.

**Priority.** High. The clearest measure of how welcoming a repository is.

---

### 9. PR Size vs Review Time

**What it would measure.** Correlation between PR size (additions plus deletions)
and time to response or merge.

**Sources.** [Keser 2026](https://armanckeser.com/writing/jellyfin-flow) on the
Batch Size Death Spiral; [Little's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little%27s_law),
where reducing batch size is one of the two levers on cycle time.

**Implementation.** A join between `fct_response_times` and `stg_pull_requests`.
Requires extracting additions and deletions, which the PR list endpoint does not
return.

**Priority.** Medium.

---

### 10. Cross-Pollination Index

**What it would measure.** Whether contributors interact with each other or
everything routes through the maintainer.

**Sources.** [CHAOSS — Contributor Absence Factor](https://chaoss.community/kb/metric-contributor-absence-factor/)
measures concentration of contributions; this would extend it to reviews and
interactions.
[Predicting OSS Sustainability (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09064v1):
"community structure plays a critical role: projects with low bus factor, high
contributor turnover, or limited repeat participation face elevated risks."

**Definition.** If contributor A comments on contributor B's PR, that is
cross-pollination. If only the owner ever comments, the bus factor of review is
one. Expressed as the share of comments and reviews that are not the owner's.

**Implementation.** A new mart crossing `stg_issue_comments` with
`stg_pull_requests` to find who comments on whose PR.

**Priority.** Medium-high. Genuinely novel — nobody publishes it.

---

### 11. Review Iterations per PR

**What it would measure.** CHANGES_REQUESTED → revision cycles per PR.

**Context.** Three to five review rounds is normal; ping-pong reviews of five or
more are the most common cause of slow cycles.
[Cortex 2026](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/ai-is-burning-out-the-people-who-keep-open-source-alive)
reports incidents per PR up 23.5% as code merges faster and review quality falls.

**Implementation.** Count state transitions in `stg_pr_reviews` per PR.

**Priority.** Medium.

---

### 12. Commits on a PR branch

**What it would measure.** Whether the author pushed changes after a review.

**Why it matters.** This is the largest known gap in Who Has The Ball. Without
it, a PR waiting for re-review is invisible in the tool whose whole purpose is
that nothing waits unseen.

**Implementation.** New extraction. GitHub's timeline endpoint would supply this
along with assignees, labels and reactions in one call.

**Priority.** High, but it is new extraction and therefore new API cost.

---

### 13. Time-of-Day Heatmap

**What it would measure.** When contributors act, and when the maintainer
responds. A signal of burnout and of timezone mismatch.

**Implementation.** Extract hour and weekday from `fct_contributor_events`.

**Priority.** Low. Good for storytelling, not actionable.

---

### 14. Conversation Depth

**What it would measure.** Back-and-forth exchanges per item.

**Implementation.** Count comments grouped by item. Partial only — GitHub's
issue comments endpoint has no thread structure, so who replies to whom is
unavailable.

**Priority.** Low.
